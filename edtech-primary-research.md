# EdTech Case Study — Primary Research (Discovery)

**Project:** New-age learning platform for non-technical professionals to get comfortable with Tech & AI
**Method:** Qualitative user interviews (open-ended, behavioral)
**Source:** Team interview observations shared in the 18 Aug group session
**Status:** Round 1 complete — feeds Problem Space PRD. Survey planned to validate at scale.

> **Read this first — scope & honesty caveats.** This is a *qualitative* round of ~12
> interviews shared in one session (individual members conducted more; not all were
> detailed). Findings below are patterns from a small, non-random sample — directional,
> not statistically representative. I have **not** attached percentages to these; with
> ~12 conversations, a number like "60% said X" would be false precision. Where I write
> "most" or "several," it reflects the interviews actually described in the transcript.
> Anything marked **[hypothesis]** is interpretation, not something a user stated. The
> planned survey exists precisely to convert these qualitative signals into something
> countable.

## 2. Core themes (what the interviews converge on)

### T1 — "I don't know where to start, or what to learn next." *(Strongest, most repeated)*
The architect (#2), the SAP switcher (#5), and the HRMS professional (#6) all named this
directly, and Shay's synthesis of six conversations landed on the same thing: people have
**no clarity on what to do first, what to do next, or how it pays off later**. Everyone
recommends a different tool (ChatGPT vs Claude vs Gemini) and the beginner can't tell which
is for them. The architect put it as: *"I need to start. I don't need to explore hundreds
of tools."*

### T2 — Technical jargon is the immediate bounce. *(Strong, very specific, actionable)*
The wealth manager (#10) closed a video after 15 minutes because of jargon he didn't
recognize; the mentor (#12) said bootcamps were unfollowable for the same reason. A
recurring refrain across the group: *"explain it like I'm in 8th class / like I'm a
seven-year-old."* One member noted Meta's chatbot was preferred for beginners precisely
because it explains concepts (even MCP, microservices) in simple, real-world terms. **The
first 15 minutes decide whether a non-technical learner stays or leaves — and jargon is
what makes them leave.**

### T3 — Passive long-form loses people; byte-sized + interactive + daily is what they ask for.
Multiple interviewees independently asked for short (10–15 min) lessons on a **daily**
basis rather than 1–2 hour videos. The Kotak PM (#11) specifically wants a **quiz/activity
inline** with the content to confirm understanding. Members recalled their own corporate
compliance courses staying engaging *because* of inline questions. This directly matches
the Duolingo-style, habit-forming mechanic in the brief.

### T4 — Learning only sticks if it attaches to a real task with somewhere to apply it.
The SEO specialist (#8) learned Google Analytics but had no work opportunity to apply it →
lost confidence and retention. The HR (#9) learned compliance frameworks but couldn't use
them with management. The wealth manager (#10) wants AI tied to *his* 12-hour workflow.
**Theory without application fails; the "win" has to land on the learner's actual job.**

### T5 — They want mentorship / personalized guidance — but can't commit to live sessions.
The architect (#2) wants live classes and a mentor to answer his exact doubts, not recorded
video. The HRMS professional (#6) valued the cohort's 45-minute hand-holding to install
Claude. **But** the same people say they have no time for scheduled webinars/Zoom — the
wealth manager (#10) noted he might show up for two weeks, then a family event breaks the
rhythm. **The want is "someone answers *my* specific doubt, on demand" — not a fixed live
timetable.** *[hypothesis: an always-available, personalized AI tutor resolves this tension
better than live cohorts.]*

### T6 — The market feels like clutter + hype + burned money → deep distrust.
The HRMS professional (#6) spent ~₹45k and now won't spend more "unless it's really
trustworthy" — there's genuine buyer's remorse. He and others describe YouTube as noise
with no way to judge quality, and courses as **push products** using FOMO/panic marketing
("if you don't learn Claude, you're behind") and ₹10-to-enroll-then-₹14k traps. Tutorials
also mislead by demoing *paid* tool versions the learner doesn't have (#8, #2). **Trust is
the scarce resource, and several buyers are already burned.**

### T7 — Non-technical professionals use AI shallowly — prompt in, output out, nothing more.
The lawyer, therapist, architect, and Reliance professional all use AI *only* for prompting
and summarization; as one put it, they don't know "what happens after the prompt, or what
other features exist." Most usage is a **faster Google search**. **This shallow-usage gap
is the actual opportunity** — not teaching AI from zero, but moving people from "I can
prompt" to "I can actually use and build with this."

### T8 — Motivation splits into "mandated" vs "self-motivated by career ROI."
- **Mandated / low-intent:** lawyer (#3 — only if the firm requires), Reliance professional
  (#7 — wouldn't use otherwise), therapist (#4 — no professional benefit). These convert
  poorly for a *voluntary* learning product.
- **Self-motivated by career ROI / high-intent:** SAP switcher (#5), wealth manager (#10),
  HR wanting a promotion (#9), architect wanting to expand skills (#2). These people have a
  trigger, intent, and willingness to pay — *if* the product delivers.

## 3. Who to serve — and who to de-prioritize

The negative signals are as useful as the positive ones for keeping scope tight:

**De-prioritize (validated by this round):**
- Professions where AI has low job-relevance or is structurally discouraged — e.g. the
  **lawyer** (billing model) and **therapist** (can't automate, no benefit). They won't
  learn voluntarily.
- **Technical/senior devs** as learners — the senior dev (#1) is capped by token budgets
  and not learning-motivated in the way this product needs.
- Anyone whose real need turned out to be a *different product* (the therapist's
  subscription-tracker) — out of scope.

**Sharpened target persona *[hypothesis, to confirm via survey]*:**
> A **non-technical professional in a tech-enabled role or company** (marketing, HR, ops,
> finance, or a career-switcher), **self-motivated by career ROI**, who is stuck at "where
> do I start," bounces off jargon, can't commit to scheduled live sessions, wants
> byte-sized daily interactive learning tied to a **real task**, and *will* pay — but only
> after trust is earned.

This is a tightening of the "AI-anxious individual contributor" (Persona A) from the
kickoff. The interviews did **not** overturn the AI-wedge bet — pain clustered squarely
around AI, not tech-in-general.

## 4. Solution signals (what users implied they'd want)

Labeled as **signals**, not decisions — these are inputs to solution design, not a locked spec.

- **Reverse the onboarding.** Shay's key insight: instead of pushing content, *first ask
  "what do you want to do with AI? what's your daily use case?"* then prescribe a **minimal**
  starting path. Personalize the journey to the learner's real task.
- **Byte-sized, daily lessons** (10–15 min) rather than long videos.
- **Near-zero jargon** — "explain it to an 8th-grader." Treat the first 15 minutes as the
  make-or-break retention window.
- **Interactive checks** — inline quiz/activity to confirm understanding (asked for
  explicitly by #11).
- **Anchor every lesson to the learner's real task** and give them a place to apply it →
  the "first win."
- **On-demand doubt resolution** (an AI tutor / mentor feel) instead of a fixed live
  timetable.
- **Proof before payment** — a free, genuine first win before any ask, to counter the
  burned-buyer distrust.
- **A curation / trust layer** — "here's the *one* path and tool for someone like you,"
  cutting the clutter and the free-vs-paid tutorial mismatch.
- **Pricing signal:** willingness-to-pay is real but conditional; the HRMS professional
  wants **case-by-case / per-use-case** pricing over a big upfront course fee.

## 5. Tensions to resolve in design (hold these honestly)

1. **"Give me a mentor / live help"** vs **"I have no time for scheduled sessions."**
   → likely resolved by *async, on-demand, personalized* help, not live cohorts.
2. **"Just tell me where to start, don't make me explore"** vs **"the journey must be
   customized to me."** → a guided onboarding that asks the use case, then prescribes a
   *single minimal* path — customized *destination*, not customized *effort*.
3. **"I'll pay for something good"** vs **"I've been burned and won't spend again."**
   → free first win / proof-first, earn the paid conversion.

## 6. How this corroborates the secondary research

The primary round strongly reinforces the desk research from the kickoff:
- The **completion crisis** (MOOCs ~5–15%; ~50% of dropouts in the first two weeks) shows
  up live as the SAP switcher's start-drop-restart cycle and the "boredom with recorded
  lectures" pattern.
- The fixes the data pointed to — **micro-learning, hands-on projects, interactivity,
  personalization** — are exactly what users independently asked for.
- The **jargon wall** is a specific, human mechanism behind the abstract "engagement" gap.

Caveat carried over: the secondary figures come from aggregators citing WEF/PwC/IDC/edX and
should be verified against primary reports before entering the PRD.

## 7. Open questions → the survey (validation plan)

The team agreed to run a survey next, log all interviews in a shared Excel, and continue
interviews + secondary research. The survey should convert the qualitative signals above
into countable evidence. Priority questions to answer at scale:

1. **Rank the pains.** Of {don't-know-where-to-start, jargon, can't-apply, drop-off,
   can't-judge-which-course}, which is #1 for the most people?
2. **Willingness to pay.** What will they actually pay, and in what shape — per-course,
   **per-use-case**, or subscription? What price point reads as "trustworthy"?
3. **Segment conversion.** Does the self-motivated, career-ROI, non-technical,
   tech-enabled segment actually convert best?
4. **Path preference.** Prescribed minimal path vs fully customized path — which do they
   want?
5. **Format confirmation.** Byte-sized daily + inline quiz — confirm demand beyond the
   dozen interviews.

**Survey mechanics (from the session):** keep questions easy; distribute across the team's
networks for volume; add an opt-in line — *"open to a 20–25 min chat? leave your email/
phone"* — to recruit deeper interviews.

## 8. Implications for the MVP (early, tentative)

*[hypothesis — for solution design, not yet decided]* An MVP that could carry all of the
above: a learner tells the product **one real task** they want to do with AI → it gives a
**jargon-free, ~10-minute guided lesson** that walks them to doing that task for real (the
first win) → an **inline check** confirms understanding → an **on-demand AI tutor** answers
their specific doubts → a **daily, byte-sized streak** brings them back. Free first win,
then a case-by-case or low-commitment paid path once trust is earned.

Keep scope brutal: **one persona, one first-win task, one loop**, built end to end — per
the brief's own warning against feature sprawl.
